How Jewellery Price Is Calculated: Gold Rate, Making Charges, Wastage and GST

Every jewellery bill is built from the same few parts. Once you can see them, any quote becomes easy to read and easy to question.

The short answer

A jewellery price is the value of the metal, plus the cost of making it, plus anything extra such as stones, plus GST. That is the whole structure. The differences between one jeweller and another come from how each part is worked out and how it is written on the bill.

The parts of a jewellery bill

  1. Metal value. Weight of the piece in grams multiplied by that day's rate per gram for the purity you are buying (22K, 18K and so on).
  2. Wastage. Some jewellers add a percentage for the metal lost while the piece is cut, melted and finished. It is sometimes called value addition or VA.
  3. Making charges. The cost of the labour and skill that went into the piece. It is quoted either as a percentage of the metal value or as a flat amount per gram.
  4. Stone and other charges. Diamonds, gemstones, pearls, enamel work or a special box are usually priced separately from the metal.
  5. GST. Added on top at the end.

1. Start with the gold rate, and ask which one it is

The rate changes through the year and can change within a day, so the same chain can cost different amounts on different dates. Two things are worth checking. First, which purity the rate is for. A 24K rate and a 22K rate are not the same number, and 22K gold is about 91.6% pure gold, so the rate you are charged for a 22K piece should be the 22K rate. Second, whether the rate on the bill is the rate of the day you are paying. If you booked or paid an advance earlier, ask how the rate was fixed.

To read the purity of a piece, look for the BIS hallmark. Hallmarked jewellery carries a mark showing the purity and a HUID code that identifies that piece, which you can use to confirm what you are buying.

2. Making charges: percentage or per gram

This is the part that varies most. A plain machine-made chain usually carries much lower making charges than an intricate bridal set, because far less skilled work goes into it. Some jewellers quote making as a percentage of the metal value, others as rupees per gram. Neither is wrong, but they behave differently. A percentage rises when the gold rate rises, while a per-gram charge stays fixed. If you are comparing two jewellers, convert both to the same basis before deciding which is lower.

There is no standard rate. Making charges depend on the design, the method of making and the jeweller, and they can often be discussed, particularly on larger purchases.

3. Wastage: ask whether it is on top of making

Wastage is meant to cover metal lost in making. Some jewellers charge it separately and some build it into the making charge. The thing to ask is simple: “Is wastage charged separately from making?” If both appear on the bill, you are paying for both. If one jeweller shows wastage and another does not, the second may simply be including it elsewhere, so look at the final total, not the line item.

4. Stones are priced on their own

Diamonds and coloured stones are usually charged by their own weight and quality, not by the gold rate. Ask for the stone weight and the stone charge as separate lines. Also remember that the stones add weight to the piece, so the metal weight should be shown separately from the gross weight.

5. GST: how it is applied

On the sale of gold jewellery, GST is 3% of the total value of the transaction, and that value includes the making charges. The GST Council's FAQ for the gems and jewellery sector gives the example directly: on a chain priced at ₹30,000 with ₹28,000 of gold and ₹2,000 of making, the 3% applies to the full ₹30,000.

A different rule applies if you give your own gold to a jeweller or workshop to have a piece made. In that case the making (job work) charge is taxed at 5%, and only on the making charge, not on your gold. This is why you may see two different GST figures in two different situations.

A worked example

Here is an illustrative 10-gram 22K gold chain. The rate and charges below are made up to show the method. They are not today's rates, so use your jeweller's actual figures.

Gold value (10 g × ₹10,000 per gram)₹1,00,000
Wastage (3%)₹3,000
Making charges (12%)₹12,000
Stone and other charges₹2,000
Subtotal₹1,17,000
GST (3% of subtotal)₹3,510
Total₹1,20,510

Notice that GST is charged on the subtotal, which already includes making and wastage. Because the percentages are applied step by step, a small change in the gold rate moves the total by more than you might expect, since wastage, making (when quoted as a percentage) and GST all move with it.

Questions worth asking before you pay

  • Which rate is this, and for which purity?
  • Is making charged as a percentage or per gram, and on what weight?
  • Is wastage charged separately from making?
  • What is the net metal weight, and what is the stone weight and stone charge?
  • Is the piece BIS hallmarked, and can I see the HUID?
  • Is GST included in the price you quoted, or added on top?

Check the maths yourself

You do not need to do this by hand. Our free Jewellery Price Calculator takes the weight, rate, making, wastage, stone charges and GST and shows the breakdown line by line. There are also versions for gold and silver. You enter the rate yourself, so use your jeweller's quoted rate or a published reference rate for the day.

If you are a jeweller

Customers trust a bill they can follow. Showing the rate, purity, net weight, making, wastage and GST as separate lines costs you nothing and removes most of the questions at the counter. Many jewellers now put a price calculator or a clear “how we price” page on their website for the same reason. Frequently asked questions

Is GST charged on making charges?

On gold jewellery sold to you, yes. The 3% GST applies to the total value including the making charges. If you give your own gold for a piece to be made, the job work charge is taxed at 5% on the making charge only.

What is the difference between wastage and making charges?

Making charges pay for the labour and skill that go into the piece. Wastage is meant to cover the metal lost while making it. Some jewellers show both, some combine them, so always compare the final total.

Why do two jewellers quote different prices for the same design?

The gold rate they use, the way they charge making (percentage or per gram), whether they add wastage, and the stone charges can all differ. Putting both quotes on the same basis shows the real difference.

What does BIS hallmark mean on jewellery?

It is a mark that shows the purity of the gold and carries a HUID code that identifies that piece. It helps you confirm that the purity you are paying for is the purity you get.

Can I negotiate making charges?

Often you can, especially on larger purchases or plain designs. The gold rate itself is usually fixed for the day, so making charges and wastage are where there may be room to talk.
